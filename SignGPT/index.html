Sign languages are visual, spatial languages with their own grammar, structure, and community. Building AI that truly serves the Deaf community requires moving beyond surface-level translation — towards systems that understand the full richness of signed communication and can generate it naturally, bidirectionally, at scale.

Overview

SignGPT is a six-year UKRI EPSRC Programme Grant (2025–2031) bringing together researchers from the University of Surrey, University of Oxford, and University College London to tackle one of the hardest open problems in AI: unconstrained, bidirectional translation between British Sign Language and English. The project spans computer vision, sign linguistics, and machine learning, and is developed in close collaboration with Deaf organisations and community partners.

The central goal is to build the first large-scale generative model for sign language — a SignGPT — trained on a dataset larger than anything previously assembled for a signed language. Rather than treating BSL as a derivative of English or reducing it to sequences of isolated signs, the project is designed around the full structure of signed communication: its spatial grammar, simultaneous multi-articulator organisation, and the breadth of variation across Deaf signers. Alongside the core model, the project is developing a Visual Language Toolkit — a suite of open tools for annotation, language learning, and automatic interpretation — intended to serve both the research community and the wider Deaf community directly.

The current state of the SLP field

Interest in the computational modelling of sign languages has accelerated considerably over the last decade, with SLP research now regularly appearing at top-tier machine learning and computer vision conferences. The field has largely concentrated on three primary tasks — recognition, production, and translation — typically operating on video data with gloss annotations or free-text translations as supervision. Sign-to-text translation is commonly treated as a pipeline problem: recognition feeds into gloss-level representations, which are then translated to spoken language text.

More recent work has introduced gloss-free approaches that model sign-to-text mapping end-to-end, without gloss intermediaries. However, both gloss-based and gloss-free paradigms tend to prioritise mapping coarse manual features to spoken language text, often without modelling the full compositional and multimodal structure of signed communication. Text-to-sign research similarly tends to prioritise surface-level appearance and reconstruction, with limited evaluation of linguistic composition. Advancing the field requires rethinking not just the models, but the data, representations, and evaluation criteria that underpin them.

Language representation

The development of SLP has been strongly shaped by the characteristics of existing datasets — which for continuous signing largely consist of interpreted broadcasts, content sourced from social media, or curated linguistic corpora. Each source carries significant limitations. Interpreted signing is produced under live broadcast constraints and differs substantially from natural Deaf communication: it routinely involves omissions, simplifications, and restructuring relative to the spoken source, and broadcast captions are not verbatim representations of what is signed. Web-sourced material raises concerns around consent, copyright, and unknown signer proficiency.

Gloss annotations — approximate word-level transcriptions of manual signs — are the dominant supervision signal in SLP, but they are both costly to produce and systematically incomplete. A single sign form can represent multiple meanings depending on context, and sociolinguistic variation means the same concept may be realised differently across signers and regions. Crucially, glosses collapse or ignore the productive constructions that are central to sign grammar: pointing, depicting signs, spatial indexing, and classifiers. Only around 15% of the BSL Corpus has been annotated at all.

Considering simultaneity, constituent order, and linear modelling paradigms

Reliance on gloss annotations also reflects a deeper structural mismatch: sign languages are not sequentially ordered in the way spoken languages are. Grammatical relations in signing are generally less dependent on strict linear sequencing — signed grammatical structures may map onto multiple spoken-language sequences, and vice versa. The linear, token-based modelling paradigms inherited from spoken-language NLP are poorly suited to this.

Linearised intermediate representations — gloss strings or caption-aligned tokens — introduce an information bottleneck that obscures spatial grammar, productive constructions, and flexible constituent ordering. Standard evaluation metrics such as BLEU fail to capture the compositional and spatial structure of signing. Progress in SLP will likely require modelling approaches that move beyond strictly sequential assumptions towards representations capable of capturing multimodality, abstract use of space, cross-articulator temporal alignment, and inductive biases informing productive language use.

Beyond the lexicon

A related challenge concerns non-lexical material. Non-manual signals — brow raises, eye gaze, mouthings, head movements, body posture — contribute to lexical, grammatical, prosodic, and discourse-level meanings simultaneously across multiple articulators. Despite their fundamental role in the grammar of sign languages, many SLP approaches focus primarily on coarse pose and motion, neglecting these cues entirely.

Work that does incorporate non-manual features into model inputs has demonstrated improved performance, but this remains relatively limited. Beyond non-manuals, productive constructions — depicting signs, spatial indexing, classifier constructions — encode information that is only weakly, if at all, captured in gloss-based annotations, where they are frequently reduced to coarse categories. Grammatical functions including directionality, clause type marking, and adverbial modification are conveyed through spatial and articulatory modulation rather than discrete lexical items. Without representing these dimensions, models risk prioritising lexical recognition at the expense of broader linguistic competence.

Towards real-world generalisation

To improve real-world applicability, SLP must move towards greater use of fluent Deaf conversational data — deaf-presented, ethically sourced, and naturalistic — supported by scalable annotation tools. Progress depends not only on dataset scale, but on modelling and evaluation approaches that reflect the multimodal, spatial, and simultaneous nature of sign language. Systems must be able to handle co-articulation, signer variation, and the full range of linguistic structures present in spontaneous signing, not just the features that are easiest to extract from interpreted broadcast material.

Within this context, the SignGPT project is developing a conversational agent incorporating grounded translation pipelines, both lexicalised and productive language structures, and attention to linguistic diversity — factors essential for systems that generalise beyond constrained benchmarks toward real-world interaction.

Visual Language Toolkit

To address these challenges, the project is developing the Visual Language Toolkit (VLT) — a modular suite of semi-automatic annotation tools designed to scale corpus development without sacrificing linguistic principles. The VLT is under active early development and will be released as open-source software, with a central design principle of interoperability with established annotation platforms such as ELAN. Early tools cover sign segmentation, sign spotting, non-manual feature tracking, and 3D signer reconstruction.

Beyond annotation, the VLT aims to support the full sign language processing pipeline — providing interfaces between visual representations, symbolic linguistic units, and large language models. Although developed primarily for sign languages, the toolkit is explicitly designed to generalise to other forms of visual and multimodal communication. Selected capabilities will be made accessible through web-based demonstrations and applications, including dictionary search tools, sign language learning and assessment tools, and conversational interfaces. The VLT is preliminary and its scope and tools will evolve as the project develops.
